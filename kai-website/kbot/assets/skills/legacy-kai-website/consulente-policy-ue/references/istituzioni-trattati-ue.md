# EU Institutions and Treaties: Foundations of European Integration

## 1. Historical Evolution of European Integration

### The ECSC and the Treaty of Paris (1951)
The European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC), established by the Treaty of Paris (1951), marked the beginning of European supranational integration. Six founding members—Belgium, France, the German Federal Republic, Italy, Luxembourg, and the Netherlands—pooled sovereignty over coal and steel production to prevent future conflict and facilitate economic cooperation. The ECSC introduced the supranational principle, where member states delegate authority to independent institutions that serve the collective European interest rather than individual national interests.

### The Rome Treaties and EEC (1957)
The Treaty of Rome (1957) expanded European integration by establishing the European Economic Community (EEC), with the same six member states. The EEC created a customs union, eliminated internal tariffs, and established the principle of a common external tariff. The treaty introduced the concept of a customs union and aimed at the progressive approximation of economic policies. Founding institutions included the Council (representing national governments), the Commission (executive and administrative body), the Assembly (later Parliament), and the Court of Justice.

### The Single European Act (1987)
The Single European Act (SEA 1987) reformed the institutional framework and introduced qualified majority voting (QMV) in the Council, removing the national veto on single market issues. The SEA's principal goal was to complete the internal market by 31 December 1992, eliminating remaining non-tariff barriers and harmonizing national regulations. This act facilitated the regulatory approximation necessary to achieve the four freedoms and established the European Political Cooperation (EPC) framework for coordinated foreign policy.

### The Maastricht Treaty and Monetary Union (1993)
The Treaty on European Union (TEU), signed in Maastricht (1992) and entered into force in 1993, fundamentally restructured the EU into three pillars: the Community (renamed European Union), Common Foreign and Security Policy (CFSP), and Justice and Home Affairs (JHA). Maastricht introduced convergence criteria for monetary union participation and established the goal of economic and monetary union (EMU) by 1999/2002. The treaty strengthened the Parliament's role through co-decision procedures and introduced the principle of subsidiarity, requiring action at the Union level only when objectives cannot be sufficiently achieved by member states.

### The Treaties of Amsterdam, Nice, and Lisbon (1997–2009)
The Treaty of Amsterdam (1997) addressed transparency, fundamental rights protection, and employment policy coordination. The Treaty of Nice (2001) reformed institutional procedures before enlargement to Central and Eastern Europe, addressing Council voting mechanisms and Commission composition. The Treaty of Lisbon (2009), which amended both the TEU and TFEU, eliminated the pillar structure, expanded Parliament's co-legislative power through the ordinary legislative procedure, and introduced the Charter of Fundamental Rights as binding law. The Lisbon Treaty also created the European Council as a formal institution and introduced the flexibility clause (Article 352 TFEU) for unforeseen circumstances.

## 2. The Treaty Framework: TEU and TFEU

### Treaty on European Union (TEU)
The Treaty on European Union defines the constitutional and political foundations of the Union. It establishes the EU's values (democracy, human rights, rule of law), principles (subsidiarity, proportionality, primacy), and institutional structure. The TEU governs the Common Foreign and Security Policy, internal security cooperation, and the relationship between the Union and member states. Key provisions include Article 2 (values), Article 4 (national identities and competences), Article 5 (subsidiarity and proportionality), and Title VII (final provisions on amendment and accession).

### Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU)
The TFEU contains the substantive policies and legal mechanisms of the Union. It is structured thematically, covering categories of competence (exclusive, shared, supporting), the internal market and the four freedoms, economic and monetary policy, and sectoral policies. Articles 3–6 TFUE define competence distribution: exclusive competence (customs union, competition, monetary policy for eurozone, commercial policy); shared competence (internal market, social policy, transport, environment, consumer protection); and supporting competence (culture, education, public health, tourism).

## 3. Institutional Architecture

### The European Commission
The Commission serves as the executive and administrative body of the Union. It comprises a President, Vice-Presidents, and Commissioners (one per member state), appointed through a joint procedure involving Parliament and the Council. The Commission proposes legislation, implements Union law, enforces competition policy, manages the EU budget, and represents the Union in international negotiations. The President is elected by the European Parliament for a five-year term.

### The Council of the European Union
The Council represents the governments of the member states and coordinates policies. It comprises ministers from the national governments, organized by policy field (e.g., Economic and Financial Affairs Council—Ecofin, General Affairs Council). The Council shares legislative power with the European Parliament under the ordinary legislative procedure and negotiates international agreements. The presidency rotates among member states every six months. Voting methods include unanimity (sensitive matters like taxation and foreign policy), qualified majority voting (QMV—the standard for most legislation), and simple majority (procedural matters).

### The European Parliament
The European Parliament, directly elected by EU citizens every five years, has 705 members distributed among 27 member states according to population. It shares legislative power with the Council under the ordinary legislative procedure (Article 289 TFEU), participates in budget approval, and elects the Commission President. The Parliament is organized into political groups (not national delegations) and thematic committees. Its powers include co-legislation on most Union matters and budgetary authority.

### The European Council
The European Council brings together heads of state or government of the member states, the Commission President, and the High Representative for Foreign Affairs. It meets at least four times per year and sets the political direction and priorities of the Union. It does not have legislative power but provides political guidance and addresses strategic challenges. The President of the European Council (distinct from the Commission President) is elected for a two-and-a-half-year term.

### The Court of Justice (CJEU)
The Court of Justice of the European Union comprises the Court of Justice, the General Court, and specialized courts. The Court of Justice has jurisdiction over actions by member states against institutions, preliminary rulings on Union law interpretation (Article 267 TFEU), and annulment of acts of EU institutions. The General Court handles actions brought by individuals and private entities against EU acts. Preliminary rulings allow national courts to request authoritative interpretation of Union law, ensuring uniform application across member states.

## 4. EU Legal System and Principles

### Direct Effect
The principle of direct effect, established in *Van Gend & Loos v. Nederlandse Administatie der Belastingen* (Case 26/62, ECJ 1963), means that Union law creates rights and obligations that individuals can invoke directly before national courts. Regulations have direct effect; directives have direct effect only if they establish clear, precise, unconditional rights after the implementation deadline (vertical direct effect against member states). Member state directives and general principles of Union law also may have direct effect under certain conditions.

### Primacy (Supremacy) of Union Law
The principle of primacy, established in *Costa v. ENEL* (Case 6/64, ECJ 1964), means that Union law takes precedence over national law, including constitutional provisions. This principle ensures the uniform application of Union law and prevents member states from using national law to escape Union obligations. The primacy principle is essential to the effectiveness of Union law and the coherence of the legal order.

### Effet Utile (Useful Effect)
The principle of effet utile, or useful effect, requires that Union law be interpreted and applied in a way that achieves its full effect and purpose. This principle guides judicial interpretation and informs member state implementation of directives. It underlies the broad interpretation of Union competences and the purposive approach to enforcement.

### Subsidiarity and Proportionality
Article 5 TEU establishes that the Union acts only where necessary (subsidiarity) and uses means proportionate to objectives (proportionality). Subsidiarity requires that action be taken at the Union level only if the objectives cannot be adequately achieved by member states alone. Proportionality requires that measures not exceed what is necessary to achieve legitimate objectives. These principles constrain Union action and protect national autonomy.

## 5. Competence Distribution

### Exclusive Competence (Article 3 TFEU)
The Union has exclusive competence in: customs union; competition rules necessary for single market functioning; monetary policy for eurozone members; conservation of marine biological resources (common fisheries policy); and common commercial policy. Member states cannot legislate in these areas.

### Shared Competence (Article 4 TFEU)
Member states and the Union share competence in: internal market; social policy; environment; consumer protection; transport; trans-European networks; energy; area of freedom, security, and justice; and public health. When the Union legislates in shared competence areas, member state competence is preempted, but member states retain residual authority in areas where the Union has not acted.

### Supporting Competence (Article 6 TFUE)
The Union has supporting competence (cannot harmonize member state laws) in: culture; education and vocational training; youth and sport; civil protection; and administrative cooperation. Union action complements rather than replaces member state authority.

## 6. Democratic Legitimacy and Decision-Making

### The Ordinary Legislative Procedure (Article 289 TFEU)
The ordinary legislative procedure (co-decision) requires agreement between the Council and the European Parliament. The Commission proposes legislation. Parliament adopts a first reading position, which the Council may accept or amend. If disagreement persists, a conciliation committee comprising Council and Parliament delegates attempts compromise. This procedure applies to the vast majority of Union legislation and ensures democratic legitimacy.

### Qualified Majority Voting (QMV)
Qualified majority voting requires a "qualified majority" of Council members for decision-making. As of 2014, a qualified majority comprises 55% of member states representing 65% of the EU population. This threshold encourages coalition-building while preventing any single member state from blocking decisions unilaterally. Enhanced cooperation procedures allow willing member states to move forward in certain policy areas despite disagreement by others.

### National Parliaments
The Lisbon Treaty strengthened the subsidiarity control mechanism, requiring the Commission to justify proposals and allowing national parliaments to monitor Union activities. National parliaments receive Union legislative proposals and may raise subsidiarity concerns if they believe the Union is overstepping its authority.
