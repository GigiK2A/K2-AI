# The Single Market and the Four Freedoms

## 1. The Concept and Architecture of the Internal Market

### Origins and Development
The internal market is the cornerstone of European integration, defined as a space without internal frontiers in which the free movement of goods, people, services, and capital is ensured in accordance with the provisions of the TFEU (Article 26 TFUE). The Treaty of Rome (1957) established the Common Market as a customs union with shared external tariff. The Single European Act (1987) accelerated completion of the internal market by 31 December 1992 through harmonization of technical standards, mutual recognition of professional qualifications, and removal of remaining fiscal and administrative barriers.

### The Four Freedoms Framework
Articles 26–207 TFUE establish the legal framework for the internal market. The four freedoms are: free movement of goods (Articles 28–37 TFUE); free movement of persons including workers and establishment (Articles 45–66 TFUE); free movement of services (Articles 56–66 TFUE); and free movement of capital and payments (Articles 63–66 TFUE). These freedoms are not absolute; Articles 36 and 45(3)–48 TFUE provide exceptions for public security, public policy, public health, and protection of morality.

## 2. Free Movement of Goods

### The Customs Union and Tariff Barriers
The EU customs union eliminates internal tariffs and quotas on goods originating in member states or in free circulation (Articles 28–30 TFUE). Goods from non-member states paying the common external tariff are then in free circulation. Member states cannot impose any charges having an equivalent effect to customs duties (Articles 28–29 TFUE). Even small administrative fees or charges on imported goods may be considered equivalent to customs duties if they have protective effect.

### Quantitative Restrictions and Equivalent Measures
Article 34 TFUE prohibits quantitative restrictions (quotas) and all measures having equivalent effect. The Court of Justice, in the landmark case *Dassonville v. Gouverneur de la Commune de Jambes* (Case 8/74, ECJ 1974), defined measures having equivalent effect as any trading rule capable of hindering intra-Union trade. This broad definition covers production standards, labeling requirements, advertising restrictions, and even national measures applied indiscriminately to domestic and imported goods if they have a disproportionate effect on imports.

### The Cassis de Dijon Principle
In *Rewe-Zentral AG v. Bundesmonopolverwaltung für Branntwein* ("Cassis de Dijon", Case 120/78, ECJ 1979), the Court established that member states may not refuse to recognize goods lawfully produced and marketed in another member state, even if they do not conform to domestic standards. This mutual recognition principle allows goods complying with standards in the country of origin to be sold elsewhere in the Union, provided they do not pose health or safety risks. The principle facilitates market integration while respecting national regulatory autonomy. However, member states may impose higher standards if justified by mandatory requirements such as consumer protection, fair trading, or environmental protection, provided restrictions are proportionate.

### Exceptions to Free Movement
Article 36 TFUE lists explicit exceptions to Articles 34–35 (goods free movement): protection of human, animal, or plant health; protection of national treasures; and protection of intellectual property. These exceptions must be applied without discrimination (Article 110 TFUE) and must not constitute a disguised restriction on trade. Member states bear the burden of justifying restrictions and proving that no less restrictive alternatives exist.

## 3. Free Movement of Workers and Persons

### The Right to Work Across the Union
Article 45 TFUE guarantees freedom of movement for workers, which encompasses the right to seek employment, to work, and to reside in another member state without discrimination based on nationality. Workers enjoy equal treatment in employment terms, pay, dismissal, and social security. Regulation (EU) 2018/1806 and the Free Movement Directive (2004/38/EC) provide the detailed framework for implementing this freedom.

### Professional Qualifications and Services
Article 48 TFUE empowers the Council to issue directives on the mutual recognition of diplomas, certificates, and other evidence of formal qualifications. Sectoral directives cover healthcare professions, engineers, architects, and other regulated professions. General systems directives allow professionals to work in regulated professions in other member states if they meet equivalent qualification requirements. The Services Directive (2006/123/EC) facilitates establishment and cross-border provision of services by streamlining authorization procedures and reducing unnecessary restrictions.

### Self-Employment and Establishment
Article 49 TFUE guarantees freedom of establishment, allowing EU nationals to set up businesses, participate in professions, or manage enterprises in other member states without discrimination. This freedom extends to companies and firms incorporated in member states, which must have the right to establish secondary branches, agencies, or subsidiaries (Article 54 TFUE). Companies and professional partnerships are treated as equivalent to natural persons for purposes of freedom of establishment.

### Limitations and Public Interest Exceptions
Articles 45(3) and 52 TFUE permit restrictions justified by public security, public policy, or public health. The Free Movement Directive (2004/38/EC) specifies that restrictions based on these grounds must be proportionate, non-discriminatory, and based on personal conduct that poses a genuine, present, and sufficiently serious threat. Simply being unemployed or a burden on social services is not grounds for expulsion. Family reunification rights are protected (Articles 2–7, Directive 2004/38/EC).

## 4. Free Movement of Services

### Scope and Definition
Article 57 TFUE defines services as activities normally provided for remuneration and not covered by other freedoms (goods, persons, capital). Services include healthcare, education, financial services, tourism, consulting, and digital services. The freedom of services encompasses both cross-border provision (provider in State A serving customers in State B) and temporary establishment (provider moves temporarily to State B to deliver services).

### Services Directive Framework
The Services Directive (2006/123/EC) eliminates obstacles to service provision and establishment. It requires member states to: simplify authorization procedures; eliminate discriminatory or unnecessary requirements; ensure point-of-single-contact procedures; and maintain quality standards. Certain sensitive services (security, healthcare administration, social services) benefit from flexibility provisions. The directive promotes mutual trust in regulatory standards, reducing the need for detailed harmonization.

### Digital Services
The Digital Single Market Directive and subsequent digital legislation extend service freedom to digital provision. The Digital Services Act (DSA, 2022) and Digital Markets Act (DMA, 2022) establish governance frameworks for online services, protecting consumer rights and fair competition. These acts require transparency, consumer protection, content moderation accountability, and interoperability standards. The E-Commerce Directive (2000/31/EC) provides the legal foundation for digital service provision across borders.

### Restrictions and Derogations
Article 62 TFUE allows restrictions on services provided for remuneration based on public policy, public security, or public health, with the same proportionality requirements as for goods and persons. Member states may impose licensing requirements if objectively necessary and applied without discrimination. However, restrictions must not obstruct the fundamental right to service provision.

## 5. Free Movement of Capital

### The Framework
Article 63 TFUE provides for the free movement of capital between member states and between member states and third countries. Capital movements include direct investment, real estate purchases, stocks and bonds, loans, credits, and insurance. Article 64 allows restrictions for third-country capital movements in certain circumstances (national security, tax evasion) but prohibits restrictions between member states.

### Financial Services Integration
The Capital Requirements Directive and Regulation (CRD/CRR) establish a unified regulatory framework for banking and financial services, allowing credit institutions and investment firms to operate across the Union with a single license. The Market Abuse Regulation (MAR) and Markets in Financial Instruments Directive (MiFID II) harmonize trading and conduct rules. These frameworks support the free movement of capital by reducing friction and regulatory divergence.

## 6. Horizontal Issues: Harmonization and Mutual Recognition

### Technical Harmonization and Standards
The Union employs both harmonization (establishing common rules) and mutual recognition (accepting equivalence of national rules) to reduce barriers. The New Legislative Framework (NLF, 2008) establishes a light-touch approach: the Union sets essential safety requirements, and manufacturers self-certify compliance using harmonized European standards. CE marking signifies compliance, allowing goods to circulate freely without national authorization.

### Regulatory Cooperation and SIEG Doctrine
The Services of General Economic Interest (SIEG) doctrine allows member states to provide universal services (postal, transportation, utilities) under public service obligations, even if they restrict competition or require subsidies. Article 106(2) TFUE exempts measures necessary to provide SIEG services from competition rules, provided they do not distort competition beyond what is necessary. This doctrine balances market integration with social objectives.

## 7. Digital Single Market and Emerging Issues

### E-Commerce and Digital Goods
The E-Commerce Directive permits distance selling across borders and limits member state authorization requirements. Digital goods (software, e-books, music) are increasingly subject to free movement principles, though geographical licensing restrictions (based on contractual arrangements) remain common. The Digital Services Act (DSA) requires large online platforms to ensure content moderation, transparency, and user protection across the Union.

### Data Flows and Privacy
The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) harmonizes data protection standards, facilitating cross-border data flows while protecting fundamental rights. Data localization requirements are prohibited except where justified by security or public interest (GDPR Article 6). Cloud services and cross-border data processing are essential to the digital single market.

### Regulatory Fragmentation and Brexit
Post-Brexit, the Union applies its single market rules to Northern Ireland (Protocol on Ireland/Northern Ireland) but the UK maintains separate regulatory regimes in Great Britain. This creates regulatory divergence, with implications for goods, services, and financial market integration. The Union continues to deepen mutual recognition agreements with third countries (e.g., Switzerland) while maintaining its own standards autonomously.
